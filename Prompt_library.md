# Exercise 1: Blog Post



# Input Prompt: 



&#x09;Write a blog post about why remote work is good.



# Chat-gpt Output:



# Why Remote Work Is Good: More Freedom, Better Focus, Stronger Teams



Remote work is more than a perk. Done well, it gives people greater control over how they work, helps organizations find talent beyond a single city, and can create a more thoughtful, flexible culture.



The most immediate benefit is time. Without a daily commute, employees can reclaim hours each week for family, exercise, rest, or simply a calmer start to the day. That time matters: people who begin work less rushed often bring more energy and focus to it.



Remote work can also improve productivity. Many kinds of work—writing, coding, analysis, planning, design, and problem-solving—benefit from long stretches of uninterrupted concentration. A quiet, personalized workspace can make those stretches easier to protect. Instead of measuring commitment by who stays latest at a desk, remote teams can focus on outcomes: the quality of the work and the results it produces.



For employers, remote work expands access to talent. A company is no longer limited to candidates who live within commuting distance of an office. That can lead to more diverse teams, broader perspectives, and a better chance of finding the right person for a role.



It also encourages clearer communication. In an office, decisions can disappear into hallway conversations. Remote teams must document plans, define responsibilities, and communicate intentionally. When that habit is strong, information becomes easier to find and collaboration becomes less dependent on being in the right room at the right time.



Of course, remote work is not automatically good. It requires trust, good tools, healthy boundaries, and deliberate ways to build connection. Some people prefer the structure and social energy of an office, and some jobs need in-person work. The goal should not be to force everyone into one model.



But when organizations give people flexibility, support, and clear expectations, remote work can make work fit life better—not the other way around.

# Exercise 2: LinkedIn post



# Input Prompt: 



&#x09;Write a LinkedIn post about learning prompt engineering.



# Chat-gpt Output:



Learning prompt engineering has changed how I use AI.



The biggest lesson: better results rarely come from “clever prompts.” They come from clear thinking.



A strong prompt usually answers:

* What do I want?
* Who is the audience?
* What context matters?
* What constraints should the output follow?
* What does a good answer look like?



Instead of asking, “Write a post about leadership,” try:



“Write a concise LinkedIn post for first-time managers about giving useful feedback. Use a practical, encouraging tone and include three actionable tips.”



The difference is clarity.



Prompt engineering is really the skill of turning an idea in your head into instructions someone—or something—can act on. That makes it valuable well beyond AI: it improves briefs, communication, and problem-solving too.



My current rule: start simple, review the output, then refine the prompt based on what is missing.



AI works best when we treat prompting as a conversation, not a one-shot command.

# Exercise 5: Youtube script



# Input Prompt: 



&#x09;Write a youtube video script about how to start a business.



# Chat-gpt Output:



Title: How to Start a Business: A Simple Beginner’s Guide



[OPENING]



Starting a business can feel overwhelming. You have an idea, but then come the questions: Where do I begin? Do I need money? What if nobody buys?



The good news is you do not need to have everything figured out before you start. You just need a clear first step.



In this video, I’ll walk you through a practical way to turn an idea into a real business.

[SECTION 1: START WITH A PROBLEM]



The best businesses solve a real problem.



Instead of beginning with, “What can I sell?” ask, “What problem can I help someone solve?”



Maybe people need healthier lunch options near their office. Maybe small businesses need help managing social media. Maybe parents want easier ways to organize family schedules.



A good business idea sits where three things meet: a problem people care about, skills or knowledge you can offer, and customers willing to pay for a solution.



[SECTION 2: KNOW YOUR CUSTOMER]



Next, get specific about who you want to help.



“Everyone” is not a target customer. Try defining a real person: their job, lifestyle, frustrations, goals, and buying habits.



For example, instead of saying, “I help businesses with marketing,” say, “I help local fitness studios attract more members through short-form video.”



The clearer your customer, the easier it becomes to create an offer, write marketing messages, and decide where to find them.



[SECTION 3: TEST BEFORE YOU BUILD]



One of the most common mistakes is spending months building something before learning whether people want it.



Start small.



Talk to potential customers. Ask about their current challenges. Show them a simple version of your idea. Create a landing page, offer a trial service, make a prototype, or take pre-orders.



You are looking for evidence, not compliments. A person saying, “That’s a great idea,” is nice. A person giving you their email, booking a call, or paying is much more meaningful.



[SECTION 4: CREATE A SIMPLE OFFER]



Your offer should answer three questions:



What do you provide?

Who is it for?

What result does it help them achieve?



For example: “I create affordable meal-prep plans for busy professionals who want to eat healthier without spending hours in the kitchen.”



Keep it simple. You can improve and expand later. At the beginning, clarity beats complexity.



[SECTION 5: HANDLE THE BASICS]



Once you have early interest, handle the practical pieces.



Choose a business name, decide on a legal structure that fits your location and situation, open a separate business bank account, and track your income and expenses from day one.



Requirements vary by country and industry, so check the rules that apply where you operate—especially for taxes, licenses, insurance, and customer data.



You do not need a perfect logo or an expensive website to begin. You do need a way for customers to understand your offer and contact or pay you.



[SECTION 6: FIND YOUR FIRST CUSTOMERS]



Your first customers often come from direct, personal outreach.



Tell people what you are building. Share useful content that addresses your customer’s problem. Reach out to potential clients with a relevant, respectful message. Ask friends and professional contacts for introductions.



Focus on one or two channels at first—such as LinkedIn, Instagram, local events, referrals, or email. Consistency in a few places is more effective than trying to be everywhere.



[SECTION 7: LEARN AND IMPROVE]



Your first version will not be perfect. That is normal.



Pay attention to what customers ask, where they hesitate, what they value most, and why they choose—or do not choose—your offer.



Use that feedback to improve your product, price, messaging, and process. Building a business is less about getting everything right immediately and more about learning quickly.



[CLOSING]



Starting a business does not require a perfect plan. It requires a real problem, a clear customer, a simple offer, and the willingness to test, learn, and improve.



Start with one conversation, one small experiment, or one offer this week.



If this video helped, like and subscribe for more practical business advice. And in the comments, tell me: what business idea are you thinking about starting?
